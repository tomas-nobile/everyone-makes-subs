    Why it works this way

    • The QR code opens the room directly and the language comes from the phone. Two seconds later you're reading, with the latest lines already loaded.
    • Languages are labeled relative to the talk ("original", "translation"), each in its own language. There is no redundant "Original / Spanish" option.
    • Translation never shows a dead screen: while a sentence is being translated, the "speaking" bars and a gray line with the original show the system is alive.
    • Newest text at the bottom, in white; older lines fade to gray. If you scroll up to reread, autoscroll stops and "Back to live" appears with the number of new lines.
    • States use plain language and never say "error". "Waiting for audio" and "Break" tell attendees what's going on and that they don't need to do anything.
    • Advanced controls stay hidden: the stream delay only appears if you turn on "I'm watching the stream".
    • "What did I miss?" serves a summary cached per room, so cost doesn't grow with the audience.

    Why it works this way

    • Two huge lines and nothing else. The top one fades to gray and the bottom one is current. In the original language the live line is added, which is the fastest text there is.
    • The corner QR code ("Captions in your language") makes the screen advertise the phone view. It's the cheapest way to get people using it.

    • A 20% margin is kept on the right for the QR code, plus safe margins for projectors that crop.

    If you see music or the presenter from another room here, the input is wrong.

    • Don't close this tab. If the laptop goes to sleep, captions stop.
    • If the internet drops, we buffer 10 seconds of audio and send it when the connection is back.

    Why a room station

    • It's the audio source for someone who isn't a developer: a laptop, a cable from the mixer and a link. RTMP and SRT live under "Advanced".
    • The link carries the room key (scanned from the dashboard), so nobody types passwords in the booth.
    • "What's being transcribed" is the most useful check: a level meter alone won't tell you that background music is coming in instead of the speaker's mic.
    • It needs HTTPS to use the microphone, which is why first-run setup configures internet access (Tailscale or Cloudflare).

    Auditorium

    Why it works this way

    • Cards instead of a 10-column table. The first thing you see is what needs attention; alerts sit at the top with the button that fixes them.
    • The last transcribed line on each card is the most useful signal: you notice right away if the room next door or music is coming in.
    • Production language, not engineering language: "No audio", "Delayed", "Break". p50/p95, rotations and cost show up under "Technical details" (for the judges and for you).
    • The schedule runs the day: each card shows the current and next talk, and switching is confirmed with one button.
    • The vocabulary shows it's working ("Kubernetes ✓ 9"). Click the Auditorium card to see it.

    Why it works this way

    • Nobody edits a .env. The desktop app opens this on first launch; in Docker it shows until a key is set.
    • "Test" makes a real call and turns errors into plain language: invalid key, project without model access, out of quota.
    • Internet access is a guided checklist, not a command: the app detects Tailscale, runs tailscale funnel --bg 8080, tells you when the permission click is needed and tests the address from outside before letting you continue.
    • The schedule is pasted as is, copied from the website. Gemini turns it into rooms and talks, and you confirm in a table before saving.
